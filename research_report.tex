\begin{abstract}
Cross-stock return forecasting is central to quantitative research in equity markets, yet practitioners and researchers face a persistent bottleneck: manually discovering which other stocks carry predictive signal for a target ticker is labor-intensive, error-prone, and rarely reproducible. Many existing approaches fix a handful of neighbors or ignore calendar alignment entirely, leading to invalid comparisons and fragile conclusions. In this paper, we introduce ECHO (Equity Cross-stock Heuristic Orchestrator), an LLM-driven agentic experimentation platform for systematic cross-stock neighbor discovery and multi-horizon cumulative return forecasting on the Indian NIFTY equity universe. The framework comprises an Orchestrator that proposes diverse neighbor sets using metadata, sector graphs, and memory of past experiments; a Forecast Service that trains a configurable supervised regressor on calendar-aligned daily returns with explicit chronological splits and naive baselines (zero-return and train-mean); an Experiment Logger that records every proposal with full traceability; and an Analyzer that aggregates results into a calibrated dashboard for honest evaluation. The same calendar-aligned feature pipeline is consumed by two complementary backbones, gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost) and a recurrent network (a stateless LSTM operating on sliding sub-sequences of the engineered tabular features), enabling like-for-like model comparison under identical splits. Rather than claiming a single superior model, ECHO treats each forecaster as a rigorous measuring instrument and automates the full research loop: propose $\rightarrow$ measure $\rightarrow$ remember $\rightarrow$ refine. Across $1{,}175$ calibrated experiments at horizon $H=10$ on $56$ NIFTY constituents, the platform shows that (i) agent-proposed neighbors clearly dominate fixed-correlation peers under the boosted-tree backbone (Ours wins on $42$ targets, target-only on $12$, fixed-correlation on $2$); (ii) under the LSTM backbone, agent-proposed neighbors and target-only features are nearly tied (Ours $25$, target-only $24$, fixed-correlation $7$), revealing that the value of cross-stock features is materially backbone-dependent; and (iii) head-to-head, the LSTM achieves the lower MAE on $35$ of $56$ targets versus $21$ for the boosted trees, while the boosted trees retain a small edge in average MAE. By turning forecasting into a repeatable, auditable, and multi-model experimentation pipeline, ECHO bridges the gap between manual workflows and production-grade quantitative research.

\section{Introduction}

Forecasting equity returns is central to quantitative research and portfolio construction, but the main bottleneck is rarely model fitting alone. In practice, the expensive part is running a disciplined research loop: proposing cross-stock hypotheses, evaluating them under leakage-safe temporal constraints, and preserving institutional memory of what worked, what failed, and under which market regime. Ad-hoc pipelines often shortcut this loop by using static neighbor rules, weak date alignment, or incomplete baseline reporting, which makes results hard to trust and harder to reproduce.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Let \(i\) be a target stock, \(H\) a prediction horizon in trading days, and \(\mathcal{S}\) the available stock universe. The task is to select a small neighbor set \(\mathcal{N}_i \subset \mathcal{S}\setminus\{i\}\) such that features derived from \(\mathcal{N}_i\), together with target-history features, improve prediction of the target's cumulative forward return
\[
R_{t:t+H}^{(i)} = \frac{P_{t+H}^{(i)}}{P_t^{(i)}} - 1
\]
relative to target-only and fixed-correlation baselines. Evaluation must remain leakage-safe: date-consistent alignment, chronological splitting, horizon-aware purging between train and test, and explicit comparison to naive zero-return and train-mean predictors.

\subsection{Research Gaps and Motivation}

Prior work has advanced model architectures, but the end-to-end neighbor-discovery workflow remains under-specified in many practical settings. The core gaps that motivate this thesis are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No systematic neighbor search loop for daily-price settings:} Many relational methods assume fixed graphs or rely on expensive auxiliary streams (news corpora, transcripts, knowledge graphs), while practical desks often start from daily OHLCV plus lightweight metadata.
    \item \textbf{Inconsistent temporal hygiene in evaluation pipelines:} Forecasting \(H\)-day forward returns requires careful date alignment and purge gaps; otherwise, row-order joins or overlapping labels can leak future information.
    \item \textbf{Limited controlled comparison across model classes:} It is often unclear whether a neighbor set is genuinely informative or merely favorable to one architecture.
    \item \textbf{Weak experiment provenance:} Reporting only best-case MAE obscures failed hypotheses, baseline-relative calibration, and robustness diagnostics.
\end{itemize}

These gaps motivate an agentic formulation: treat neighbor discovery as an iterative, evidence-driven process of proposal, measurement, logging, and refinement.

\subsection{The ECHO Framework Overview}

To address these gaps, we introduce ECHO (Equity Cross-stock Heuristic Orchestrator), an agentic experimentation system for cross-stock return forecasting on a NIFTY-derived universe. ECHO combines four components: (1) an Orchestrator that proposes neighbor sets, (2) a Forecast Service that evaluates each proposal under a fixed leakage-safe protocol, (3) an Experiment Logger that stores full run provenance, and (4) an Analyzer that aggregates logs into comparable diagnostics and leaderboards.

The proposal stage is grounded in structured context rather than free-form prompting. A metadata layer separates externally observed descriptors (e.g., sector, industry, market capitalization, beta) from derived market signals (e.g., momentum, volatility, drawdown, trend regime, and correlation statistics), and a weighted similarity graph provides a structural prior for candidate selection. The Orchestrator also consults top historical configurations and, when available, summarizes recent headline sentiment as additional context.

The evaluation stage is deliberately strict and model-agnostic. For each proposal, the Forecast Service constructs the supervised table on target-indexed trading dates, reindexes neighbor returns to the same date axis, and drops invalid rows after lag/rolling transformations. It then applies a chronological 80/20 split with a horizon purge (train end at \(\text{split}-H\)) so that training labels cannot overlap the test window. Each run reports MAE against two explicit naive baselines (zero-return and train-mean), and configurations that fail to beat zero receive a ranking penalty in the calibrated leaderboard.

This thesis evaluates ECHO with two backbones under the same data pipeline: a gradient-boosted tree regressor (XGBoost) and a stateless LSTM trained on sliding windows of the engineered tabular features. On the \(H=10\) benchmark, the calibrated log contains \(1{,}175\) experiments over \(56\) unique symbols in our NIFTY universe (the count exceeds 50 because of constituent turnover in the study window). Under boosted trees, Proposed neighbors win on \(42/56\) targets (A: \(12\), B: \(2\)); under the stateless LSTM, Proposed and target-only are nearly tied (\(25\) vs \(24\), with B at \(7\)). The key empirical result is therefore methodological: neighbor value is real but backbone-dependent.

From a search perspective, the hypothesis space is combinatorial. For universe size \(N\) and neighbor count \(k\), each target has \(\binom{N-1}{k}\) candidate sets; with \(N=56\) and \(k=3\), this is \(26{,}235\) combinations per target. ECHO addresses this by replacing one-shot heuristics with a measured propose-measure-remember loop under identical scoring conditions.

Finally, ECHO adopts request-scoped retraining. Because the neighbor set changes feature identity, reusing a checkpoint trained on a different neighbor schema is not semantically valid. Retraining per proposal keeps comparisons fair across hypotheses and preserves traceability across both backbones.

\subsection{Novel Contributions}

The novelty of this thesis is \emph{process-centric}, not architecture-centric. We do not claim a new universal forecasting model; instead, we contribute a rigorous way to discover and validate cross-stock predictors under realistic data and evaluation constraints. The core novelty is concentrated in the following points:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Closed-loop agentic neighbor discovery.} We formalise neighbor selection as an iterative propose-measure-remember-refine loop, where each proposal is generated from structured evidence (metadata, similarity graph, and experiment memory) and then executed under the same forecast protocol.
    \item \textbf{Leakage-safe, baseline-calibrated measurement for neighbor search.} We enforce date-aligned feature construction, chronological splitting with horizon purge, and mandatory zero-return/train-mean baselines, together with a baseline-aware ranking rule. This turns neighbor comparison into a calibrated scientific test rather than an isolated MAE report.
    \item \textbf{Backbone-controlled attribution of relational value.} We evaluate the same neighbor hypotheses on the same engineered dataset under two model classes (XGBoost and stateless LSTM). This isolates whether gains come from better neighbors or from architecture-specific inductive bias.
    \item \textbf{Auditable experimentation as a first-class output.} We treat full experiment provenance (rationale, neighbors, horizon, backbone, holdout metrics, and stability diagnostics) as a research artifact, enabling reproducible analysis and cumulative institutional memory across runs.
\end{enumerate}

Supporting modules such as metadata enrichment, graph construction, and dashboard visualization are important enablers, but the principal novelty claim of this thesis is the \emph{methodological framework} for reliable cross-stock neighbor discovery.

\subsection{Thesis Organization}

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. \textbf{Section 2} reviews related work in classical equity forecasting, relational financial time‑series models, and agentic LLM‑based forecasting systems, highlighting the specific gaps that ECHO addresses. \textbf{Section 3} presents the ECHO framework in detail: metadata construction, similarity graph logic, the Orchestrator’s multi‑stage search, the Forecast Service with leakage‑safe evaluation, the Experiment Logger, and the Analyzer dashboard. \textbf{Section 4} describes the experimental setup, data collection, feature engineering, and reports results at horizons \(H=10\) and \(H=20\), including Baseline A/B/Proposed comparisons, head‑to‑head XGBoost vs. LSTM benchmarks, sector influence analysis, and error analysis. \textbf{Section 5} concludes the thesis, summarizes key findings, discusses limitations, and outlines directions for future work.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Classical and Machine-Learning Equity Forecasting}

Equity return forecasting has long been central to empirical asset pricing and quantitative portfolio construction. Classical studies explain expected returns through cross-sectional factors such as size, value, and momentum \citep{fama1993common,jegadeesh1993returns}. More recent work shows that machine-learning models can exploit high-dimensional firm characteristics and nonlinear interactions that are difficult to capture with linear factor models \citep{gu2020empirical,chen2023deep}. These advances are important, but they largely treat each observation as a firm-date feature vector and do not make the discovery of cross-stock predictors a first-class research object.

This distinction matters because financial return forecasting is dominated by low signal-to-noise ratios. A method can report a visually small improvement in mean absolute error (MAE) and still fail to beat a zero-return or historical-mean baseline; conversely, a seemingly large MAE can be meaningful when it is measured on multi-day cumulative returns and consistently improves on strong naive baselines. For this reason, ECHO evaluates every proposed neighbor set against explicit zero-return and train-mean baselines under chronological splits, rather than presenting a standalone error number.

\subsection{Relational Financial Time-Series Forecasting}

A separate line of literature argues that assets should not be modeled as isolated stochastic processes. Economically linked firms exhibit predictable return spillovers because information diffuses gradually through supply chains, customer relationships, sectors, and investor attention networks \citep{cohen2008supply,menzly2010market}. Graph-based stock-prediction models operationalize this idea by representing firms as nodes and inter-firm dependencies as edges \citep{matsunaga2019graph,li2020modeling}. These models are the closest methodological context for ECHO because they also attempt to extract predictive signal from relationships among equities.

The relational literature is not homogeneous. The reported numbers across papers are not directly comparable because the papers optimize different target variables, horizons, and market universes: continuous forward-return regression, next-day directional classification, overnight movement classification, and cross-sectional ranking are mathematically different tasks. A fair comparison therefore requires grouping papers by target formulation and by operational burden. Table~\ref{tab:related-work-comparison} summarizes the most relevant paradigms.

Within each comparable group, the reported winner differs. For directional classification, TRACE reports the strongest signal-quality and backtest profile among the cited classification-style systems, while LSTM-RGCN shows a narrower improvement over sequence-only baselines. For cross-sectional ranking and portfolio construction, MDGNN and MGGAL represent the highest-capacity graph-learning family, with MDGNN offering the strongest reported predictive/backtest profile and MGGAL emphasizing stability across multi-frequency markets. For continuous return regression, ECHO should not be compared to those ranking or classification metrics directly; its contribution is a leakage-safe measurement framework showing that agent-proposed neighbors materially help boosted trees on NIFTY while exposing that the same neighbors are less useful for the LSTM backbone.

These papers show that relational information can improve financial forecasting, but they also expose a trade-off. End-to-end dynamic GNNs and symbolic knowledge-graph systems can achieve strong benchmark results when rich data streams are available, yet they introduce heavy engineering requirements: intraday bars, news or transcript processing, dynamic graph construction, and careful temporal knowledge maintenance. ECHO takes a different position. It does not claim that tabular XGBoost or a stateless LSTM is universally more expressive than a dynamic graph network. Instead, it asks a narrower and practically important question: when a trading desk has daily OHLCV history and basic metadata, can an agent systematically discover useful cross-stock neighbors, test them honestly, and retain experiment memory without building a high-friction streaming graph system?

The answer is evaluated through ECHO's Baseline A/B/Proposed design. Under the boosted-tree backbone, agent-proposed neighbors outperform both target-only features and fixed-correlation peers on most NIFTY targets at horizon $H=10$. Under the LSTM backbone, proposed neighbors and target-only features are nearly tied, while fixed-correlation peers remain weakest. This is not a failure of the relational idea; it is evidence that the value of a neighbor set is backbone-dependent. Prior graph papers primarily improve the model architecture. ECHO instead improves the research process around neighbor hypothesis generation, leakage-safe measurement, and reproducible comparison.

\subsection{Agentic and LLM-Driven Approaches for Time Series and Financial Forecasting}

Large language models have recently been applied to time series forecasting by reprogramming pretrained LLMs or by representing numerical sequences as language-like tokens \citep{jin2023time,zhou2023}. These systems are useful as model-centric forecasters, but they usually do not automate the broader experimental workflow required in quantitative research: proposing feature hypotheses, enforcing calendar alignment, comparing against naive baselines, logging failed trials, and reusing empirical memory.

The rise of multi-agent systems has opened new possibilities for analytical workflows. Frameworks such as CAMEL \citep{li2023camel} and AutoGen \citep{wu2023autogen} demonstrate collaborative LLM agents for reasoning, planning, and tool use. Most relevant to our setting, TimeSeriesScientist \citep{zhao2025timeseriesscientist} introduces an LLM-driven agentic framework for general time-series analysis with specialized agents for curation, planning, forecasting, and reporting. However, it targets general forecasting workflows and does not focus on cross-stock neighbor discovery, market-calendar alignment, horizon purging, or baseline-aware ranking in equity return prediction.

ECHO advances this line of research by turning cross-stock forecasting into a closed-loop experimental-science process: propose candidate neighbors, measure them under identical leakage-safe conditions, log every outcome with rationale, and feed the measured evidence back into future proposals. This positioning also clarifies ECHO's real-world value. It is not presented as a universal alpha engine; rather, it is a deployable and auditable research infrastructure for discovering when relational features help, when they fail, and which model class can actually use them.


\section{ECHO Framework}

ECHO acts as a human quantitative researcher, systematically performing hypothesis generation, rigorous evaluation, logging, and refinement by leveraging LLM reasoning abilities. ECHO integrates four specialized components that work in concert: (1) the Orchestrator proposes diverse neighbor sets using metadata, sector graphs, and memory of past experiments; (2) the Forecast Service builds calendar-aligned datasets and evaluates a configurable supervised regressor (gradient-boosted trees or a stateless LSTM) under identical splits and explicit baselines; (3) the Experiment Logger records every run with full provenance, including the model backbone used; and (4) the Analyzer aggregates the append-only log into a calibrated dashboard that exposes per-backbone leaderboards and head-to-head comparisons. This design transforms cross-stock return forecasting into an adaptive, reproducible, and interpretable experimentation pipeline.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let \( r_t^{(i)} = \frac{P_t^{(i)} - P_{t-1}^{(i)}}{P_{t-1}^{(i)}} \) denote the daily return of stock \( i \) at time \( t \). Given a target stock \( i \), a prediction horizon \( H \), and a candidate set of neighbor stocks \( \mathcal{N} \), the goal is to forecast the \( H \)-day cumulative forward return \( \hat{R}_{t:t+H}^{(i)} \), defined as the total return from holding the stock for \( H \) trading days:
\[
R_{t:t+H}^{(i)} = \frac{P_{t+H}^{(i)}}{P_t^{(i)}} - 1.
\]
Each hypothesis is scored on a strict chronological holdout set using mean absolute error (MAE). MAE is the average absolute difference between predicted and realized cumulative returns across all test days:
\[
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}|} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}} \left| R_{t:t+H}^{(i)} - \hat{R}_{t:t+H}^{(i)} \right|.
\]
Lower MAE indicates forecasts that are closer to realized returns on average. The absolute value ignores whether a prediction was too high or too low on a given day; for example, if the actual return is $+5\%$ and the prediction is either $+2\%$ or $+8\%$, the per-day error is $3\%$ in both cases. Using signed errors instead would allow opposite misses to cancel: a $+5\%$ over-prediction and a $-5\%$ under-prediction would average to $0\%$ despite two $5\%$ mistakes. MAE prevents this cancellation when aggregating performance over the holdout set.

MAE is an \emph{evaluation} metric only. During training, both backbones optimize a signed regression loss (squared error): gradient-boosted trees use the default squared-error objective and the LSTM minimizes mean squared error. Predictions therefore remain signed quantities (e.g.\ $+4\%$ or $-2\%$); absolute values are applied only when scoring the holdout set for logging, comparison, and neighbor ranking. The latest-point forecast returned by the service preserves this sign as \texttt{predicted\_return}.

\subsection{Metadata Construction and Similarity Graph Logic}

Before orchestration begins, ECHO creates a metadata object for each stock. For each symbol \(s\), close prices are read from a locally cached historical price tape, while selected fundamentals (sector, industry, market capitalisation, beta) are fetched from a public market-data provider. These fundamentals are not predicted by the model; they are external descriptors used for hypothesis generation.

\paragraph{Return-loading protocol.}
For each stock, the service ingests the raw price records, retaining the pair \((\text{Date},\text{Close})\) as the observed market series. Dates are parsed as calendar timestamps and closes are coerced to real-valued prices; malformed entries are mapped to missing values and excluded. The cleaned table is then indexed by \(\text{Date}\), which enforces a time-ordered, calendar-consistent representation for all downstream joins and lag operations. Daily arithmetic returns are computed as
\[
r_t^{(s)} = \frac{P_t^{(s)}}{P_{t-1}^{(s)}} - 1.
\]
Because the first timestamp has no predecessor \(P_{t-1}^{(s)}\), it is removed by construction, and the function outputs a date-indexed univariate return series \(\{r_t^{(s)}\}\).

\paragraph{Forecast-service views of the search space.}
The orchestrator-facing services expose four complementary views of the candidate space, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:forecast-endpoints}.

The metadata and candidate views define the raw pool; the graph view injects structural priors; the best-neighbor history provides empirical evidence from prior runs. A separate qualitative-context channel (market news sentiment) contributes a contemporaneous signal that is not present in the price tape.

Let \(P_t^{(s)}\) be close price and \(r_t^{(s)} = \frac{P_t^{(s)} - P_{t-1}^{(s)}}{P_{t-1}^{(s)}}\). The metadata pipeline computes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Volatility (30-day)}: \(\sigma_{30}^{(s)} = \operatorname{std}(r_{t-29:t}^{(s)})\), using the latest rolling window.
    \item \textbf{Momentum}: \(m_k^{(s)} = \frac{P_t^{(s)}}{P_{t-k}^{(s)}} - 1\), for \(k \in \{20, 60\}\).
    \item \textbf{Relative Strength vs NIFTY}: \(RS_t^{(s)} = \frac{\prod_{u \le t}(1 + r_u^{(s)})}{\prod_{u \le t}(1 + r_u^{(\text{NIFTY})})}\), computed on date-aligned returns.
    \item \textbf{Drawdown metrics}: with rolling 252-trading-day high \(M_t^{(s)} = \max_{u \in [t-251, t]} P_u^{(s)}\), drawdown is \(DD_t^{(s)} = \frac{P_t^{(s)} - M_t^{(s)}}{M_t^{(s)}}\). Then \texttt{max\_drawdown\_1y} is \(\min_t DD_t^{(s)}\), and \texttt{current\_drawdown} is the latest \(DD_t^{(s)}\).
    \item \textbf{Trend regime}: using \(MA_{50}\) and \(MA_{200}\), we assign \texttt{Bullish} if \(P_t > MA_{50} > MA_{200}\), \texttt{Bearish} if \(P_t < MA_{50} < MA_{200}\), and \texttt{Sideways} otherwise.
\end{itemize}

Cross-sectional buckets are assigned using \texttt{percentile\_bucket()} over all stocks in the current universe:
\[
\pi_s(x) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \mathbb{1}[x_j < x_s],
\]
then mapped by thresholds \([0.8, 0.5, 0.2, 0)\) to ordered labels. This yields \texttt{market\_cap\_bucket} (\texttt{UltraMega}/\texttt{Mega}/\texttt{Large}/\texttt{Mid}) and \texttt{volatility\_bucket} (\texttt{High}/\texttt{Medium}/\texttt{Low}/\texttt{VeryLow}).

Correlation features are computed at two temporal scales. First, static all-period correlation from \texttt{returns.corr()} provides \texttt{top\_correlated} (top-5 peers). Second, rolling 60-day and 120-day correlations provide \texttt{rolling\_corr\_60d} and \texttt{rolling\_corr\_120d} (top-3 peers each), capturing short-term relationship shifts. After all per-stock attributes are calculated, ECHO constructs similarity sets by exact label matching: \texttt{same\_market\_cap\_bucket}, \texttt{same\_volatility\_bucket}, and \texttt{same\_trend\_regime}.

The graph builder then constructs an undirected weighted graph \(G=(V,E)\), with one node per stock. For pair \((i,j)\), the edge weight is:
\[
w_{ij} = 0.4\,I^{\mathrm{corr}}_{ij}\,\rho_{ij}
      + 0.2\,I^{\mathrm{sec}}_{ij}
      + 0.2\,I^{\mathrm{vol}}_{ij}
      + 0.2\,I^{\mathrm{cap}}_{ij}
\]
where \(I^{\mathrm{corr}}_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[j \in \mathcal{C}_i]\), \(I^{\mathrm{sec}}_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[\mathrm{sector}_i = \mathrm{sector}_j]\), \(I^{\mathrm{vol}}_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[\mathrm{volBucket}_i = \mathrm{volBucket}_j]\), and \(I^{\mathrm{cap}}_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[\mathrm{capBucket}_i = \mathrm{capBucket}_j]\). Here \(\mathcal{C}_i\) is \(i\)'s \texttt{top\_correlated} list and \(\rho_{ij}\) is return correlation. The maximum theoretical weight is 1.0, and an edge is added only when \(w_{ij} > 0\). This graph becomes a structural prior that helps the Orchestrator discover non-obvious but quantitatively grounded neighbors.

\subsection{Orchestrator}

The Orchestrator is the hypothesis-search policy of ECHO. For each request \((i,H,b)\), where \(i\) is the target ticker, \(H\) is horizon, and \(b\) is model backbone, it first constructs a structured context by concurrently querying the forecast-service views in Table~\ref{tab:forecast-endpoints}. Concretely, it retrieves target metadata \(m_i\), a de-duplicated candidate pool \(\mathcal{U}_i\), graph priors \(\mathcal{G}_i\), and horizon-scoped memory \(\mathcal{M}_{i,H}\) of historically best neighbor sets. In parallel, it builds a qualitative context channel by collecting recent target-specific news headlines and mapping them to a compact sentiment object (discrete label, semantic summary, and a continuous market-mood index in \([0,1]\)). The planner therefore reasons over a mixed evidence tuple
\[
\mathcal{C}_{i,H,b}=\left(m_i,\mathcal{U}_i,\mathcal{G}_i,\mathcal{M}_{i,H},s_i\right),
\]
where \(s_i\) denotes the sentiment summary.

Implementation-wise, ECHO keeps the planner provider-pluggable, but the configuration described and reported in this manuscript uses OpenAI GPT models throughout: GPT-5.4 generates neighbor proposals and GPT-5.4-mini maps recent RSS headlines to the sentiment object. Typed outputs are enforced through schema validation before execution, so every proposal has a stable structure regardless of prompt variability.

Given \(\mathcal{C}_{i,H,b}\), the Orchestrator invokes the planner to generate proposals of the form
\[
e=(\texttt{experiment\_id},\,\mathcal{N}_e,\,\phi_e),\qquad |\mathcal{N}_e|\le 3,
\]
where \(\mathcal{N}_e\) is the proposed neighbor set and \(\phi_e\) is a free-text rationale. Each proposal is evaluated by the Forecast Service through \texttt{/run-forecast} with explicit provenance fields (source, round index, rationale, experiment id), so every decision remains auditable in the experiment log.

Memory injection is two-tiered. Long-term memory comes from \texttt{/best-neighbors/\{target\}?horizon=H}, which queries the horizon-specific experiment log, filters by target, canonicalizes neighbor sets, keeps the best MAE per unique set, and returns the top historical configurations. As a result, each memory element carries not only neighbors and MAE but also ranking MAE, baseline outcomes, CV diagnostics (including worst-split date interval), backbone id, rationale text, round/source tags, and timestamp metadata from the append-only log. Short-term memory is produced online within a request: the full outcomes of round 1 and the best configurations from rounds 1--2 are explicitly re-injected into later prompts. In code terms, the planner is conditioned on fields equivalent to \texttt{Past Successful Tickers}, \texttt{Best from Round 1}, \texttt{All Round 1 Results}, \texttt{Best from Round 2}, and \texttt{Historical Context}.

Search proceeds in three stages, with three structured proposals generated per stage: (i) \emph{exploration}, which samples diverse hypotheses from \(\mathcal{U}_i\) and \(\mathcal{G}_i\) while referencing long-term memory; (ii) \emph{refinement}, which conditions on complete round-1 outcomes and CV-risk diagnostics to suppress unstable patterns; and (iii) \emph{mutation}, which perturbs strong seeds with controlled cross-sector substitutions while preserving historically robust motifs from memory. Candidate ranking uses the same operational objective as the logging layer,
\[
\operatorname{score}(e)=\texttt{mae\_for\_ranking}(e)\;\text{(fallback: MAE)},
\]
and the best configuration after each stage is fed back as memory for the next stage. This closes a request-level loop that is algorithmically simple but methodologically important: neighbor discovery is no longer a one-shot prompt, but an evidence-guided sequential search with explicit feedback and reproducible traces.

\subsection{Forecast Service}

The Forecast Service is the measuring instrument of ECHO. Given a target stock \(i\), a neighbor set \(\mathcal{N}_i\), a horizon \(H\), and a model backbone \(\mathcal{F}\in\{\text{XGB},\text{LSTM}\}\), it builds a leakage-aware supervised-learning dataset and evaluates the chosen backbone under a strict chronological holdout. The dataset construction below is shared by both backbones; the only difference is whether the engineered feature matrix is consumed as a tabular block (XGB) or as a sliding sequence tensor (LSTM, described in Section~\ref{sec:data-eng}).

\paragraph{Dataset construction.}
Let \(r_t^{(i)}\) denote target daily return and \(r_t^{(j)}\) denote neighbor \(j\) daily return. Feature engineering is:
\begin{align*}
x_{t,\ell}^{\text{target-lag}} &= r_{t-\ell}^{(i)}, \quad \ell \in \{1,2,3,4,5\}, \\
x_t^{\text{target-mean10}} &= \frac{1}{10}\sum_{u=0}^{9} r_{t-u}^{(i)}, \\
x_t^{\text{target-std10}} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{9}\sum_{u=0}^{9}\left(r_{t-u}^{(i)}-x_t^{\text{target-mean10}}\right)^2}.
\end{align*}

For each valid neighbor \(j \in \mathcal{N}_i\), two cross-stock features are added after calendar reindexing to the target date index:
\begin{align*}
x_t^{j,\text{lag1}} &= r_{t-1}^{(j)}, \\
x_t^{j,\text{roll5}} &= \frac{1}{5}\sum_{u=1}^{5} r_{t-u}^{(j)}.
\end{align*}

The supervised target is the \(H\)-day cumulative forward return:
\[
y_t = \frac{P_{t+H}^{(i)}}{P_t^{(i)}} - 1,
\]
which represents the total return from holding the stock for \(H\) trading days.
Rows with undefined lag/rolling/future terms are dropped, producing \((X, y)\).

This design encodes two complementary effects: (i) target autoregressive structure (lags + local regime statistics), and (ii) neighbor spillover signals. Financially, if a related stock receives new information at \(t-1\), part of the adjustment may transmit to the target around \(t\), which motivates explicit lag-1 cross-stock terms and short rolling summaries rather than many raw higher-order neighbor lags.

\paragraph{Leakage-safe chronological split and baselines.}
For \(n=|X|\), define split index \(s=\lfloor0.8n\rfloor\). To prevent horizon-overlap leakage (since \(y_t\) depends on prices up to \(t+H\)), training ends at \(s-H\):
\[
\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}} = \{1,\dots,s-H\},
\qquad
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}} = \{s,\dots,n\}.
\]
The holdout error is MAE. Two naive baselines are reported:
\begin{align*}
\text{MAE}_{0} &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}} |R_{t:t+H}^{(i)} - 0|, \\
\text{MAE}_{\mu} &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}} |R_{t:t+H}^{(i)} - \bar{y}_{\text{train}}|.
\end{align*}

The ranking score used by the experimentation loop is
\[
\text{MAE}_{\text{rank}}=
\begin{cases}
\text{MAE}, & \text{if } \text{MAE}<\text{MAE}_0,\\
\text{MAE}+\lambda, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad \lambda=1.0.
\]
This penalty prevents weak models (worse than zero-return guessing) from dominating neighbor selection. The zero-return baseline for cumulative returns is \(R_{t:t+H}^{(i)} = 0\) (no price change over the horizon), and its MAE equals the mean absolute \(H\)-day cumulative return of the test period. As \(H\) increases, \(\text{MAE}_0\) grows (roughly proportional to \(\sqrt{H}\) under random walk), making the zero-return bar easier for the model to beat at longer horizons.

\paragraph{Time-series cross-validation.}
Beyond a single holdout, ECHO runs an expanding-window time-series cross-validation with \(n_{\text{splits}}=2\) and a purge gap \(g=H\). For each fold \(k\), since \(y_t\) depends on prices up to \(t+H\), the training labels must not span beyond the test-period start:
\[
\max(\mathcal{T}^{(k)}_{\text{train}}) + H < \min(\mathcal{T}^{(k)}_{\text{test}}),
\]
which explicitly blocks look-ahead overlap between train labels and test period. Fold MAEs \(\{m_k\}\) yield:
\[
\mu_{\text{cv}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_k,
\qquad
\sigma_{\text{cv}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(m_k-\mu_{\text{cv}})^2}.
\]
In addition, the service logs the worst fold
\[
m_{\max}=\max_k m_k
\]
and its test-date interval. This directly exposes regime fragility (e.g., stress periods) instead of reporting only average performance.

\paragraph{Operational interpretation of \(\sigma_{\text{cv}}\).}
Because equity returns are non-stationary, fold-wise dispersion is a deployment risk signal: low \(\sigma_{\text{cv}}\) indicates stability across regimes; high \(\sigma_{\text{cv}}\) indicates time-dependent behavior. In this context, optimizing only a ``best split'' is statistically unsafe, since it encourages neighbor choices that were merely lucky in one window.

\paragraph{End-to-end forecast routine.}
The endpoint executes: (1) dataset construction, (2) minimum-sample validation, (3) leakage-safe 80/20 evaluation, (4) baseline scoring, (5) MAE and ranking penalty computation, (6) time-series CV diagnostics (optional), (7) retraining on all available data for latest-point forecast, and (8) structured logging. The final prediction uses the full-fit model (for maximal data usage), while MAE metrics remain tied to strict out-of-sample evaluation.

\subsection{Experiment Logger}

The Experiment Logger is the persistence layer that turns transient forecasts into an auditable experimental record. For each \texttt{/run-forecast} call, the service creates one immutable entry and appends it to a horizon-specific JSON log (\texttt{experiments\_h\{H\}.json}; with a backward-compatible default file when no run id is provided). Concurrency is handled by an explicit file lock on a companion lock file before read-modify-write, ensuring that parallel orchestrator workers cannot interleave writes or silently corrupt the log under multi-process execution.

Each entry stores three classes of information. First, it stores the hypothesis identity: target symbol, canonicalized neighbor set (sorted lexicographically so equivalent sets share a representation), forecast horizon, model backbone, source endpoint, round index, experiment id, and free-text rationale. Second, it stores evaluation outcomes: holdout MAE, final latest-point prediction, naive baseline MAEs (zero and train-mean), beat-zero indicator, and ranking MAE after baseline penalty. Third, it stores reliability diagnostics and data lineage: CV mean/dispersion/worst-split statistics, train/test sample counts, train/test date ranges, UTC timestamp, and run id. Formally, each record can be viewed as
\[
\ell = (\,\underbrace{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{hypothesis}},\underbrace{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{evaluation}},\underbrace{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{diagnostics+lineage}}\,),
\]
with append semantics over time.

This logging policy is central to the scientific claims of ECHO. Because no entry is overwritten and every proposal carries both rationale and measured outcome, the system supports exact post-hoc reconstruction of search trajectories (which hypotheses were tried, in what order, under which backbone, and with what regime sensitivity). The same log also powers horizon-filtered memory retrieval (\texttt{/best-neighbors}) and all downstream analyzer aggregates, so exploration, evaluation, and reporting remain coupled by a single source of truth.

\subsection{Analyzer}

The Analyzer is a batch post-processing stage that converts the append-only experiment log into a calibrated analysis snapshot consumed by the dashboard. It operates on calibrated records and computes: (i) the best-per-target leaderboard at a fixed horizon, (ii) predictor frequency, (iii) the sector influence matrix, (iv) target--predictor network statistics, (v) per-target MAE distribution, (vi) backtest overlays, and (vii) per-backbone aggregates and head-to-head comparisons that drive the XGBoost-vs-LSTM section of the dashboard.

For sector influence, if \(\mathcal{M}_{a\rightarrow b}\) is the multiset of MAEs from experiments where a neighbor from sector \(a\) predicts a target in sector \(b\), then:
\[
\overline{m}_{a\rightarrow b} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{a\rightarrow b}|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{a\rightarrow b}} m,
\qquad
\text{score}_{a\rightarrow b}=100\cdot\frac{\overline{m}_{\text{global}}-\overline{m}_{a\rightarrow b}}{\overline{m}_{\text{global}}}.
\]
Higher score implies better-than-global-average predictive utility for that sector pairing.

For the network view, each directed edge \((u,v)\) (predictor \(u\) to target \(v\)) stores count and average MAE:
\[
c_{uv}=\#\{\text{experiments with }u\in\mathcal{N}_v\},
\qquad
\overline{m}_{uv}=\frac{1}{c_{uv}}\sum m_{uv}.
\]

Backtest series are regenerated with the same leakage-safe feature and split logic used by the forecast service, ensuring visualization consistency with logged evaluation methodology.


\section{Experiment}

In this section, we present a comprehensive experimental evaluation of ECHO. We describe the data collection process, exploratory data analysis, feature engineering pipeline, and then report results at both \(H=10\) and \(H=20\): per-backbone Baseline A/B/Proposed comparisons, head-to-head XGBoost-vs-LSTM benchmarks, sector-level cross-stock structure, and detailed error analysis. All experiments use real daily return data from NIFTY 50 constituents with properly calendar-aligned chronological holdouts and explicit naive baselines.

\subsection{Data Collection}

We collect daily adjusted close prices for NIFTY 50 constituents over 2019--2024 from a public market-data provider, retaining only the trading date and adjusted close per symbol; non-trading days are naturally excluded. Each symbol is also annotated with externally observable descriptors (sector, industry, market capitalisation, beta), which are stored alongside the price tape and used downstream as metadata priors rather than as model inputs.

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis}

The metadata pipeline computes several derived signals for each stock:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Volatility (30-day)}: Rolling standard deviation of daily returns.
    \item \textbf{Momentum (20/60-day)}: Price change ratios over short and medium horizons.
    \item \textbf{Relative Strength vs NIFTY}: Cumulative return ratio against the benchmark.
    \item \textbf{Drawdown metrics}: Maximum drawdown over 252 days and current drawdown from rolling high.
    \item \textbf{Trend regime}: Classified as Bullish, Bearish, or Sideways based on MA(50) and MA(200) positions.
    \item \textbf{Cross-sectional buckets}: Market cap and volatility percentile rankings across the universe.
\end{itemize}

Correlation analysis reveals structured patterns: Financial Services stocks exhibit high within-sector correlation (avg $\rho=0.72$), while Consumer Cyclical stocks show weaker intra-sector relationships (avg $\rho=0.45$). The weighted similarity graph, combining correlation strength with sector, volatility-bucket, and market-cap-bucket agreement, identifies economically meaningful neighbor candidates.

\subsection{Data Processing and Featurization}
\label{sec:data-eng}

This subsection describes how the daily price tape is transformed into a leakage-safe, calendar-aligned supervised-learning problem that is shared by both backbones (boosted trees and the stateless LSTM). We first state the pipeline as a sequence of explicit operations, then walk through a small worked example, and finally describe the model-specific reshape applied at the end.

\paragraph{Step 1: ingestion and cleaning.}
For each symbol \(s\), the raw daily history is read from disk and trimmed to the pair \((\text{Date}_t^{(s)}, P_t^{(s)})\), where \(P_t^{(s)}\) is the adjusted close. Dates are parsed as calendar timestamps and prices are coerced to real values; rows with missing or malformed entries are discarded. The cleaned table is indexed by \(\text{Date}\), enforcing a time-ordered representation for all subsequent operations.

\paragraph{Step 2: simple daily returns.}
Daily arithmetic returns are computed with one explicit shift,
\begin{equation}
r_t^{(s)} \;=\; \frac{P_t^{(s)}}{P_{t-1}^{(s)}}-1,
\label{eq:daily-return}
\end{equation}
and the first row, which has no \(P_{t-1}^{(s)}\), is dropped. This produces a date-indexed univariate return series per symbol.

\paragraph{Step 3: target-side feature block.}
For target \(i\) we compute five autoregressive lags and two short-window summaries of the target's own return path:
\begin{align}
x_{t,\ell}^{\text{tgt-lag}} &= r_{t-\ell}^{(i)}, \qquad \ell\in\{1,\dots,5\}, \label{eq:tgt-lags}\\[2pt]
x_t^{\text{tgt-mean10}} &= \frac{1}{10}\sum_{u=0}^{9} r_{t-u}^{(i)}, \label{eq:tgt-mean}\\[2pt]
x_t^{\text{tgt-std10}}  &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{9}\sum_{u=0}^{9}\left(r_{t-u}^{(i)}-x_t^{\text{tgt-mean10}}\right)^{2}}. \label{eq:tgt-std}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Step 4: neighbor-side feature block.}
For each neighbor \(j\in\mathcal{N}_i\), the neighbor's return series \(\{r_t^{(j)}\}\) is reindexed onto the target's date index (so misaligned trading days do not silently shift values across symbols), and two cross-stock features are appended,
\begin{equation}
x_t^{j,\text{lag1}} = r_{t-1}^{(j)},\qquad
x_t^{j,\text{roll5}} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{u=1}^{5} r_{t-u}^{(j)}.
\label{eq:nbr-features}
\end{equation}
Neighbors with insufficient history or no overlap are dropped from \(\mathcal{N}_i\) before features are written.

\paragraph{Step 5: cumulative forward return as the supervised target.}
The supervised target is the \(H\)-day cumulative forward return,
\begin{equation}
y_t \;=\; \frac{P_{t+H}^{(i)}}{P_t^{(i)}}-1,
\label{eq:cumulative-target}
\end{equation}
which is computed once on the target series so that every arm in the Baseline A/B/Proposed comparison sees an identical date range. Rows in which any lag, rolling, or future term is undefined are then dropped, yielding the final design pair \((X, y)\) with rows ordered by trading date.

\paragraph{Step 6: chronological 80/20 split with horizon purge.}
For \(n=|X|\), the split index is \(s=\lfloor 0.8\,n\rfloor\). To prevent the test labels from peeking at train labels through the cumulative-return horizon, training ends \(H\) rows earlier:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}}=\{1,\dots,s-H\},\qquad
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}=\{s,\dots,n\}.
\label{eq:purged-split}
\end{equation}

After applying Eq.~(\ref{eq:daily-return}) we obtain three date-aligned return series, one per symbol. Calendar alignment is enforced by an inner join on the trading date, so a row is kept only when all three symbols traded that day. For an arbitrary kept row \(t\), the design vector materialises as
\[
\mathbf{x}_t \;=\;
\big[\,
\underbrace{r_{t-1}^{(\text{ICI})},\ldots,r_{t-5}^{(\text{ICI})}}_{\text{Eq.~(\ref{eq:tgt-lags})}},\
\underbrace{x_t^{\text{tgt-mean10}},\,x_t^{\text{tgt-std10}}}_{\text{Eqs.~(\ref{eq:tgt-mean})--(\ref{eq:tgt-std})}},\
\underbrace{r_{t-1}^{(\text{HDFC})},\,x_t^{\text{HDFC,roll5}}}_{\text{Eq.~(\ref{eq:nbr-features})}},\
\underbrace{r_{t-1}^{(\text{KOTAK})},\,x_t^{\text{KOTAK,roll5}}}_{\text{Eq.~(\ref{eq:nbr-features})}}
\,\big],
\]
and the supervised label is
\(
y_t = P_{t+10}^{(\text{ICI})}/P_t^{(\text{ICI})}-1.
\)
The number of features is therefore \(p = 5 + 2 + 2|\mathcal{N}_i|\); for two neighbors this gives a row of \(11\) numbers. The per-row dimensionality scales linearly with \(|\mathcal{N}_i|\), which is the precise mechanism by which the agent's neighbor proposals enter the model.

\paragraph{Backbone-specific representation.}
Both backbones consume the same \((X,y)\) produced above; they only differ in how each row is presented to the optimiser.

\emph{Boosted trees.} XGBoost is fit directly on the tabular block \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\); each row is treated as an independent observation and tree splits are evaluated coordinate-wise on the engineered features. The default training objective is squared error; holdout quality is still reported with MAE (Section~3.1). No additional reshape is required.

\emph{Stateless LSTM.} The LSTM operates on sliding sub-sequences of the same engineered features. With sequence length \(L=5\) (chosen to match the target lag depth, so the temporal context is consistent across backbones), the input at position \(t\) is the tensor
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_t^{\text{seq}} \;=\;
\big[\mathbf{x}_{t-L+1},\,\mathbf{x}_{t-L+2},\,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_t\big]
\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{L\times p}.
\label{eq:lstm-window}
\end{equation}
Features are standardised using statistics estimated only on the training partition,
\(
\tilde{x}_{t,\ell,k}=(x_{t,\ell,k}-\mu_k^{\text{train}})/\sigma_k^{\text{train}},
\)
to prevent test-side leakage of scale information. The network is trained by minimizing mean squared error on the training partition; holdout quality is reported with MAE (Section~3.1). The network reads \(\mathbf{X}_t^{\text{seq}}\), takes the last hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\), and produces the prediction \(\hat{y}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{h}_t + b\). Crucially, the LSTM is \emph{stateless} across requests: hidden state is not carried across forecast calls or across heterogeneous neighbor sets, because changing \(\mathcal{N}_i\) changes the per-row feature identity (Eq.~\ref{eq:nbr-features}). The first \(L-1\) rows of any partition lack a full window and are handled by holding the first valid prediction constant rather than discarding the row, so the train/test split sizes remain identical across backbones.

\paragraph{Why this design supports apples-to-apples comparison.}
Because both backbones see (i) the same calendar-aligned date index, (ii) the same engineered feature columns produced by Eqs.~(\ref{eq:tgt-lags})--(\ref{eq:nbr-features}), (iii) the same cumulative-return label from Eq.~(\ref{eq:cumulative-target}), and (iv) the same horizon-purged 80/20 split from Eq.~(\ref{eq:purged-split}), any difference in MAE between the two arms is attributable to the model class and not to data, leakage, or pre-processing. This is the property we exploit in Sections~\ref{sec:xgb-vs-lstm-h10} and~\ref{sec:xgb-vs-lstm-h20} when we report head-to-head per-target comparisons and in Sections~\ref{sec:baseline-abc} and~\ref{sec:baseline-abc-h20} when we run Baseline A/B/Proposed under each backbone.

\subsection{Experimentation and Results}

We evaluate ECHO on the NIFTY 50 universe at two settled horizons, \(H=10\) and \(H=20\) trading days. Each horizon is tracked in its own experiment file, analysed independently, and visualised in the dashboard. Holdout MAE on the chronological test set (Section~3.1; split defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:purged-split}) is the sole \emph{reported} evaluation metric throughout; models are trained with squared-error loss as described above.

\subsubsection{Sector-level structure of cross-stock predictability}
\label{sec:sector-influence}

Warmer cells indicate that experiments using a predictor from sector \(a\) to forecast a target in sector \(b\) had MAE materially below the global average; cooler cells indicate the opposite.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

We present detailed error analysis through case studies and systematic examination of failure modes.

\subsubsection{Case Studies}

We present two contrasting case studies at \( H = 10 \) to illustrate both the strengths and limitations of the framework.

\paragraph{Success case: ICICIBANK (Financial Services).} The Orchestrator proposed \{HINDUNILVR, INDUSINDBK, SBILIFE\} — a mix of Consumer Defensive, Financial Services, and Insurance peers. This achieved MAE=0.0267, beating target-only (0.0273), fixed correlation (0.0596), and the zero-return baseline. The low MAE dispersion across experiments (std=0.0008) indicates stable, reproducible signal. The chosen neighbors span multiple sectors, suggesting the agent captured macro-financial liquidity proxies rather than narrow banking contagion.

\paragraph{Challenge case: TRENT (Consumer Cyclical).} Despite ECHO winning over both A (0.0628) and B (0.1283) with MAE=0.0625, the absolute error remains high (6.3\% cumulative 10-day return). The MAE dispersion across experiments (std=0.039) indicates regime-dependent performance — the model sometimes finds a useful neighbor set and sometimes does not, even for the same target. This reflects the inherent difficulty of forecasting high-beta consumer cyclical stocks, where sentiment shifts and idiosyncratic news dominate statistical structure.


Figure~\ref{fig:sector-influence} and the \(H=20\) matrix discussion in Section~\ref{sec:sector-influence-h20} jointly confirm these observations quantitatively: at \(H=10\), Industrials\(\rightarrow\)Communication Services (\(+37.6\)) and Basic Materials\(\rightarrow\)Communication Services (\(+31.4\)) are among the strongest pairs, while at \(H=20\) the weakest cell is Consumer Cyclical\(\rightarrow\)Energy (\(-80.49\)), with Consumer Cyclical self-prediction still strongly negative (\(-35.49\)). This is consistent with the TRENT challenge case above and with the broader cyclicals fragility pattern.

\subsubsection{Failure Mode Analysis}

The primary failure modes observed across experiments include:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High-volatility targets}: Stocks with volatility bucket ``High'' (e.g., TRENT, TMPV) consistently show elevated MAE regardless of neighbor selection. The best achievable MAE for these targets (0.06--0.08) is 2--3$\times$ worse than stable large-caps.
    
    \item \textbf{Regime shifts}: The CV worst-split analysis reveals that 2020 Q1--Q2 (pandemic shock) and 2022 H2 (rate-hike regime) produce systematically higher MAE across all targets. Neighbor sets that perform well in normal regimes often fail during these periods.
    
    \item \textbf{Sector isolation}: Consumer Cyclical self-prediction is the single worst cell of the sector influence matrix at \(-49.6\), and Industrials self-prediction is also strongly negative (\(-30.5\)), suggesting these sectors' returns are driven by idiosyncratic factors not captured by peer relationships.

    \item \textbf{Backbone-specific neighbor benefit}: A configuration that wins under boosted trees does not automatically win under the LSTM. At \(H=10\), agent-chosen neighbors strongly dominate target-only features under boosted trees (\(42\) vs \(12\) wins) but are nearly tied under the stateless LSTM (\(25\) vs \(24\)); at \(H=20\), Proposed remains strongest under both backbones (XGB: \(37\) wins, LSTM: \(28\) wins), but the margin structure differs by model class. The binding constraint therefore depends on backbone inductive bias as well as horizon.

    \item \textbf{Shared hard targets}: A subset of high-volatility targets (e.g., TRENT, TMPV, TMCV) sits at high MAE under \emph{both} backbones across horizons, indicating that the lagged-return feature basis itself is the binding constraint for these tickers rather than the model class. Even where the LSTM wins more often at \(H=20\) (Table~\ref{tab:xgb-vs-lstm-h20}), these names remain error-heavy and motivate future work on alternative data sources (order-flow proxies, fundamentals, richer event/news representations).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}

In this work, we presented ECHO (Equity Cross-stock Heuristic Orchestrator), an LLM-driven agentic experimentation platform designed to automate the discovery and rigorous evaluation of cross-stock neighbor relationships for daily return forecasting on the Indian NIFTY equity universe. ECHO integrates four components: an Orchestrator for intelligent neighbor proposal, a Forecast Service that fits either a gradient-boosted tree regressor or a stateless LSTM regressor on calendar-aligned features under identical splits, an Experiment Logger for complete provenance tracking, and an Analyzer that produces an interactive calibrated dashboard. Together they close the loop of propose--measure--remember--refine.

Across calibrated experiments at \(H=10\) and \(H=20\) on \(56\) NIFTY constituents, ECHO produced three substantive empirical findings. First, the platform consistently improves search quality over raw proposal noise: at \(H=10\), only \(15.15\%\) of individual runs beat the zero baseline while \(48.21\%\) of best-per-target configurations do; at \(H=20\), the corresponding values are \(24.41\%\) and \(55.36\%\). This gap is the empirical signature of the propose--measure--remember loop. Second, in Baseline A/B/Proposed comparisons, agent-proposed neighbors remain the strongest arm across horizons: for XGB, Proposed wins \(42/56\) targets at \(H=10\) and \(37/56\) at \(H=20\); for the stateless LSTM, Proposed wins \(25/56\) at \(H=10\) and \(28/56\) at \(H=20\). Third, in direct backbone benchmarks the LSTM is stronger on these logs at both horizons and especially at \(H=20\): it wins \(35/56\) targets at \(H=10\) and \(45/56\) at \(H=20\), while also achieving lower average MAE at each horizon. The fixed-correlation arm remains consistently weak, which reinforces that \emph{which} neighbors are chosen is consequential, not just whether neighbors are added.

Beyond predictive performance, ECHO's primary contribution lies in methodological discipline and transparency. By enforcing calendar alignment, horizon-purged chronological splitting, full experiment logging with backbone provenance, and side-by-side dashboard reporting, the platform addresses several common pitfalls in quantitative research pipelines. The dual-backbone design adds a second axis of comparison--feature design vs.\ model class--so that observed gains can be attributed cleanly rather than confounded.

Several limitations remain. The current feature set relies on lagged returns and rolling statistics, which already captures much of the predictable structure; this likely caps the marginal gain available from neighbor enrichment on some targets and motivates richer features (fundamentals, order-flow proxies, event/news representations). Sector diagnostics also show persistent cyclicals fragility across horizons: Consumer Cyclical self-prediction is strongly negative at both \(H=10\) and \(H=20\), and at \(H=20\) the Consumer Cyclical\(\rightarrow\)Energy cell is the most adverse pairing in the matrix. Full walk-forward validation, transaction-cost modelling, and portfolio-level evaluation remain on the roadmap, as does a systematic study of how the marginal value of cross-stock features evolves with horizon and with stronger sequence backbones.

Overall, ECHO provides a practical and extensible foundation for reproducible quantitative research in equity markets. By keeping the data pipeline, baselines, and evaluation protocol shared across backbones, and by reporting wins and losses per backbone honestly, we hope this work encourages further development of agentic systems that prioritise methodological rigour and transparency over overstated predictive claims.
